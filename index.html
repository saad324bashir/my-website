The Word Behind the Journey Junoon
The divine madness of longing.

Not madness. Not chaos. Something more specific than that.

The Sufi poets named it centuries ago — the compulsion that arrives before thought does, that moves through mystics and wanderers and anyone who has ever felt called toward something they couldn't rationally explain. They didn't treat it as a flaw to be corrected. They treated it as the closest thing a human being has to a compass.

It's the feeling in your chest before your mind gets involved. Heat. A pull toward something you can't fully name. Your fingertips itching to move before reason has had a chance to object. An unconscious purpose — the kind that doesn't ask for permission and doesn't wait for the right moment.

It rages — and burns everything you love. It smothers — under weight, under years, under days that look the same. Or it finds fuel. And it burns clean.

What remains is the quality of what you burned and how well you tended it.

And some fires don't need more fuel...
they need a spark.

That moment of ignition when you remember what it felt like to be fully alive — what actually drives you underneath everything you've built on top of it.

That's what the Karakoram does. It doesn't hand you anything. It just removes every comfortable reason not to feel it.

Islamabad to Skardu. Fifteen days.

The Karakoram Highway runs 1,300 kilometers from Punjab through the valleys of Kashmir, over Babusar Top at 4,173 meters, north through Hunza to Khunjerab Pass at the Chinese border — the highest paved international crossing on earth — then south to Skardu at the base of K2. The road took twenty years to build and cost nearly a thousand lives in construction alone. From ancient fortresses guarding the pass for centuries to some of Pakistan's 7,000 glaciers — more than any country outside the polar regions — the sheer scale of a land layered in history will shock you all from your seat.

With 360 views of some of the world's tallest mountains, you'll cross a corridor that has been moving people for over a thousand years. Traders, pilgrims, wanderers. It has always drawn those who cannot stay still.

Before You Apply

Is this for you?

For you if
You ride. You hold a valid motorcycle license and have real experience — not expert level, honest level. You've been on roads that weren't designed for comfort.
You feel, somewhere underneath the busyness, that something is missing. You can't fully name it. That's a reasonable place to start.
You are physically capable of long days at altitude. Not an athlete — someone who doesn't quit when things get uncomfortable.
You are curious about Pakistan beyond what you've been shown. You suspect the real version is nothing like the headlines.
You can sit with silence. You can be in a small group for two weeks without needing to perform.
You understand this trip will be tough. The reward is proportional to that.
Not for you if
You want a packaged, frictionless holiday. There are excellent companies that offer that. This is not one of them.
You don't hold a license or have no prior riding experience. We cannot safely take beginners on these roads.
You're coming primarily to collect content. The phones come out for the mountains. They stay down for the conversations.
You need certainty that nothing will go wrong. Certainty is not available on the Karakoram. Competent guidance is.
You're not open to being changed by it.

How Did We
Get Here?

I grew up Pakistani-American in the deep south post-9/11 — which meant spending a long time feeling like I belonged fully to neither world, before realizing that was exactly the point. Being a bridge between identities teaches you things about connection that belonging to only one side never could.

For about five years I went looking — hard. Hiked the Darién Gap solo. Fished commercially in Alaska. Hitchhiked through the Sudanese revolution. Went to the places most people don't go, for reasons I couldn't fully explain at the time.

Then I cooled down. Focused on other things. Started asking why I'd done any of it.
