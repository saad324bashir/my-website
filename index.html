The Word Behind the Journey Junoon
The divine madness of longing.

Not madness. Not chaos. Something more specific than that.

The Sufi poets named it centuries ago — the compulsion that arrives before thought does, that moves through mystics and wanderers and anyone who has ever felt called toward something they couldn't rationally explain. They didn't treat it as a flaw to be corrected. They treated it as the closest thing a human being has to a compass.

It's the feeling in your chest before your mind gets involved. Heat. A pull toward something you can't fully name. Your fingertips itching to move before reason has had a chance to object. An unconscious purpose — the kind that doesn't ask for permission and doesn't wait for the right moment.

Everyone has it. Most people don't tend it.

It rages — and burns everything you love. It smothers — under weight, under years, under days that look the same. Or it finds fuel. And it burns clean.

In Pakistan, a campfire means two things simultaneously: survival and hospitality. You build one to stay warm. You build one to welcome strangers. The fire that keeps you alive is the same fire you share with everyone who arrives.

That's not a coincidence. That's a philosophy.

The smoke that rises is the byproduct — of burning, of purifying, of becoming. White and clean or dark and heavy. Either way it dissipates. It always does.

And even when everything turns to ash — that's not the end. The Romans knew it. Mix ash with lime and you get concrete that outlasts empires. Your ruins are not a conclusion. They're material. Something harder and more permanent gets built from what burned.

What remains is the quality of what you burned and how well you tended it.

And some fires don't need more fuel...
they need a spark.

That moment of ignition when you remember what it felt like to be fully alive — what actually drives you underneath everything you've built on top of it.

That's what the Karakoram does. It doesn't hand you anything. It just removes every comfortable reason not to feel it.

Islamabad to Skardu. Fifteen days.

The Karakoram Highway runs 1,300 kilometers from Punjab through the valleys of Kashmir, over Babusar Top at 4,173 meters, north through Hunza to Khunjerab Pass at the Chinese border — the highest paved international crossing on earth — then south to Skardu at the base of K2. The road took twenty years to build and cost nearly a thousand lives in construction alone. From ancient fortresses guarding the pass for centuries to some of Pakistan's 7,000 glaciers — more than any country outside the polar regions — the sheer scale of a land layered in history will shock you all from your seat.

With 360 views of some of the world's tallest mountains, you'll cross a corridor that has been moving people for over a thousand years. Traders, pilgrims, wanderers. It has always drawn those who cannot stay still.

Before You Arrive

Pakistan

There is a version of Pakistan you have probably seen — in headlines, in briefings, in the careful warnings of people who have never been. Set it aside.

The Pakistan you will ride through is something else entirely.

Hospitality here is not etiquette. It is sacred. A stranger at your door is not an inconvenience — they are an obligation, an honor, a reason to bring out the good chai. You will be invited in before you have said a word. You will be fed before you have asked. You will be offered more than people can afford to give, and the giving will be effortless.

Once while I wandered the tight alleyways of Lahore's Old City, the sudden monsoon downpours flooded every street in just minutes. Running desperate for cover, I was pulled into a carpet shop by a henna-red bearded old man. Moments later, a blanket covered me and a hot cup of chai was placed in my hands. Two hours of conversation, walking me through every carpet, every pattern, every family that had made one, the years of labor it took. I lost count of the number of times I declined his offer of a free carpet before I was able to escape. This isn't unusual. It's the norm. It's the culture.

In the north, it gets more specific.

Between the twisting spines of three mountain ranges — Karakoram, Himalaya, Hindu Kush — each valley is its own world. Different languages, different music, different food, different ways of being. Shina and Burusho and Khowar and Wakhi spoken within hours of each other on the same road. Ismaili and Sunni communities and ancient Buddhist rock carvings in the same stretch of valley. Chitrali music that sounds like nothing else on earth. Hunzai apricots drying on rooftops in the autumn sun. Yak meat, if you're willing.

A vendor in Karimabad handed me cherries off his own tree before I'd looked at a single thing for sale. You will get stuck in traffic in the middle of nowhere behind a flock of fat-tailed sheep, and somehow leave having drunk more chai than you thought your body could hold. In Skardu the mountains are so close and so large that everything happening below them looks like it belongs to a different scale of reality. It probably does.

This is the country you will ride through. Not despite its complexity — because of it.

Every expedition we run has two routes. The physical one — Islamabad to Skardu, roughly 1,300 kilometers. And the one that runs parallel to it, inside each rider.

We don't call it a retreat. We don't call it therapy. We don't run a structured program. We create conditions: physical difficulty, unfamiliar landscape, genuine hardship, evenings around a table when people are too tired to pretend. What emerges from that is the rider's own business.

People come back from these roads changed. Not because of a designed intervention — but because the Karakoram at altitude, in a small group, without the escape routes of ordinary life, tends to do that. It has been doing that to travelers for centuries.

At Khunjerab Pass we build in a moment of deliberate stillness. Not for the photograph — everyone takes the photograph. For the question. You'll know it when it arrives.

Questions

Is Pakistan safe?
Pakistan has a reputation that doesn't match the reality of where we're going. Gilgit-Baltistan — the northern region this expedition rides through — is among the most visited and welcoming parts of the country. Tourist arrivals have increased significantly in recent years. We monitor conditions continuously and work with local partners who know this terrain intimately. If you're reading this and part of you is hoping it's a little dangerous — you're wrong. But we like you. You're probably the right person for this trip.
What riding experience do I need?
A valid motorcycle license is required — no exceptions. Beyond that, we're looking for honest self-assessment over impressive credentials. You should be comfortable on roads that aren't smooth, in conditions that aren't predictable, for long days at altitude. When in doubt, tell us the truth on the application. We'd rather help you prepare than have the wrong person on the road.
What bike will I be on?
All riders are on 150cc bikes provided through Adventure Planners Pakistan, who have over ten years of experience on this specific route. These bikes are purpose-selected for the Karakoram — nimble, reliable, and well-suited to the road conditions. A mechanic rides along in the support vehicle for the full 15 days.
What fitness level is required?
You don't need to be an athlete. You need to be someone who doesn't quit when things get uncomfortable. Beyond the riding, the expedition includes optional hikes ranging from moderate to tough — some of the most jaw-dropping views on the entire trip are only accessible on foot. A basic cardio base helps at altitude.
Do I need my own gear?
Helmet and jacket are mandatory on the road. Riding boots and protective pants are strongly recommended. You can bring your own or we can advise on rental options. Custom Junoon Journeys riding jackets are in development — available to rent or purchase. Gear is not included in the expedition price.
What's the weather like?
September and October are the best months to ride this route. Days are warm and clear, nights cool at altitude. Hunza in full autumn colour is one of the most visually striking things you will see in your life. At Khunjerab Pass at 4,693m it will be cold regardless — bring layers, seriously. A full packing guide is included in the information packet.
Do I need a visa?
Most nationalities require a visa to enter Pakistan. Many countries are eligible for visa on arrival or e-visa. We offer visa processing assistance at additional cost — note your nationality on the application and we'll advise. Start early. Pakistani visa processing times vary and we don't want it to be the thing that stops you.
What if something goes wrong mechanically?
A 4x4 support vehicle with an experienced mechanic follows the group throughout the entire route. Adventure Planners Pakistan has been running these roads for over a decade — mechanical issues are anticipated, not emergencies. You will not be left on the side of a mountain pass alone.
Can I join solo?
Yes. Most riders join solo. The group dynamic is part of the experience — you will not feel alone for long.
What is the group like?
Up to 10 riders per expedition. We select based on fit, experience, and group balance — not first come first served. You won't all be the same type of person. That's intentional.
When will I hear back?
Within 5 business days. Every application is reviewed personally. Contact: saad@junoonjourneys.com
I've never traveled to Pakistan — what do I need to know?
More than we can fit here. Accepted riders receive a detailed information packet covering Pakistani culture, history, the north specifically, food, language basics, how to dress respectfully, and everything practical you need before arrival. The short version: be curious, be humble, say yes to the chai.

How Did We
Get Here?

I grew up Pakistani-American in the deep south post-9/11 — which meant spending a long time feeling like I belonged fully to neither world, before realizing that was exactly the point. Being a bridge between identities teaches you things about connection that belonging to only one side never could.

For about five years I went looking — hard. Hiked the Darién Gap solo. Fished commercially in Alaska. Hitchhiked through the Sudanese revolution. Went to the places most people don't go, for reasons I couldn't fully explain at the time.

Then I cooled down. Focused on other things. Started asking why I'd done any of it.

I always felt that pull to just move, to experience and live. Sometimes regular life — with all its restrictions, the logic and reason we tell ourselves — isn't enough. The courage to live on my own terms, to learn, create and connect. That was it.

The answer kept coming back to the same word.

The Word

The Sufi poets had a name for it. The divine madness of longing. The thing that drives you toward something before reason has a chance to talk you out of it. I realized that everything I'd done had been an expression of Junoon — unrefined, compulsive, pointed in every direction at once. The question became: what does it look like when you refine it? When you point it somewhere intentional?

Pakistan kept pulling me back. Not just because of my roots — because of what the place actually is. A land that has been connecting people across this corridor for over a thousand years. Three mountain ranges meeting in one country. Dozens of languages, cultures, and ways of living within a single day's ride of each other. A people who turned hospitality into something closer to sacred law. And a road — the Karakoram Highway — that the world built at enormous cost because the connection it created was worth it.

Junoon Journeys came out of that. Not as a travel company — as an expression of what I believe connection actually means. To the land, to the culture, to the people you encounter, to the people riding beside you, and eventually — if the road does what I believe it does — to yourself.

Creating communities in places people didn't know could hold them. That's the mission.
