How Did We
Get Here?

I grew up Pakistani-American in the deep south post-9/11 — which meant spending a long time feeling like I belonged fully to neither world, before realizing that was exactly the point. Being a bridge between identities teaches you things about connection that belonging to only one side never could.

For about five years I went looking — hard. Hiked the Darién Gap solo. Fished commercially in Alaska. Hitchhiked through the Sudanese revolution. Went to the places most people don't go, for reasons I couldn't fully explain at the time.

Then I cooled down. Focused on other things. Started asking why I'd done any of it.

I always felt that pull to just move, to experience and live. Sometimes regular life — with all its restrictions, the logic and reason we tell ourselves — isn't enough. The courage to live on my own terms, to learn, create and connect. That was it.

The answer kept coming back to the same word.

جنون

The Word

The Sufi poets had a name for it. The divine madness of longing. The thing that drives you toward something before reason has a chance to talk you out of it. I realized that everything I'd done had been an expression of Junoon — unrefined, compulsive, pointed in every direction at once. The question became: what does it look like when you refine it? When you point it somewhere intentional?

Pakistan kept pulling me back. Not just because of my roots — because of what the place actually is. A land that has been connecting people across this corridor for over a thousand years. Three mountain ranges meeting in one country. Dozens of languages, cultures, and ways of living within a single day's ride of each other. A people who turned hospitality into something closer to sacred law. And a road — the Karakoram Highway — that the world built at enormous cost because the connection it created was worth it.

Junoon Journeys came out of that. Not as a travel company — as an expression of what I believe connection actually means. To the land, to the culture, to the people you encounter, to the people riding beside you, and eventually — if the road does what I believe it does — to yourself.

Creating communities in places people didn't know could hold them. That's the mission.

The Karakoram Ignition is the first one.
